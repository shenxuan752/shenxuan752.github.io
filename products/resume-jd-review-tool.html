Resume × JD Fit Review and Cover Letter Tool

Application workflow tool that evaluates candidate-role fit and generates targeted cover letters with explicit anti-cliché constraints

AI Agents
Workflow Tool
Anthropic Claude
Productivity Systems
Published

May 6, 2025

Type: Product · Domain: Job application workflow / candidate-role fit Stack: Python · Flask · Anthropic Claude API · pdfplumber · python-docx · Docker · Render Status: Deployable web app · Role: Builder

One-line summary

A Flask-based workflow tool that parses a JD and a resume (PDF or DOCX), evaluates candidate-role fit by identifying the role’s actual business problems and matching them against the candidate’s strongest evidence, then generates a cover letter with strict anti-cliché constraints — designed for signal, not for prettier generic text.

Why I built this

Generic AI cover-letter tools write the way AI cover-letter tools write. Abstract phrasing. Endless em-dashes. Openings that could apply to any company. Sentences that stack three “intersection of X / Y / Z” clauses. The output looks polished and reads as obviously generated — exactly the wrong signal for senior roles.

The problem isn’t the writing step. The problem is the thinking step. Most tools jump straight from “JD + resume” to “write a cover letter,” which means the model has to invent fit on the fly. The result is text that gestures at fit instead of demonstrating it.

I wanted a tool that did the analytical work upstream: identify the role’s two or three actual business problems, identify the two or three strongest pieces of evidence from the resume that prove the candidate can solve them, decide a central story in one sentence, and then write — stripping everything that doesn’t serve that story.

What I built

A Flask web app that:

  • Accepts a JD (paste or URL) and a resume upload (PDF parsed via pdfplumber, DOCX via python-docx)
  • Calls Claude with a structured system prompt that forces the model to do the analytical work before generation — silently identify the role’s biggest business problems, pick matching resume evidence, decide a central story, strip everything else
  • Returns a cover letter in standard business-letter format with explicit constraints: name the company in the opening paragraph, no “I am excited to apply,” at most one em-dash in the whole letter, no abstract phrases like “sits at the intersection,” “growth enablement,” “portfolio economics,” “at scale,” “evolving X stack”
  • Has a domain-specific variant for financial services, credit risk, risk analytics, and data science roles — emphasizes credit judgment, business tradeoffs, and cross-functional trust as evidence, rather than tools and model architectures
  • Deploys to Render via Dockerfile + render.yaml — one-click hosted

How it works

The bulk of the engineering is in the system prompt. The prompt enforces:

  1. Pre-writing analysis (silent, model-internal): identify the 2-3 biggest business problems the role is hiring to solve, identify the 2-3 strongest resume signals, decide the central story
  2. Strict format: standard business-letter greeting + 4-5 short paragraphs + sign-off
  3. Opening discipline: must name the company in the first paragraph, must connect the company’s specific business or problem to the candidate’s experience, must not be reusable for any other company
  4. No resume regurgitation: the cover letter adds interpretation, prioritization, motivation, and fit — it doesn’t restate the experience the resume already lists
  5. Anti-cliché constraints: explicit blocklist of phrases the model loves to use, hard limit of one em-dash in the entire letter, no list-style sentences
  6. Domain branching: FS/credit-risk roles use the FS-specific prompt that emphasizes business judgment and tradeoffs, not technical tools

The model can be re-run on individual paragraphs for refinement without regenerating the whole letter.

My role

Sole builder. The Flask + parsing layer was straightforward (a day). The system prompts were 80% of the work — tuning each constraint, testing against multiple JD types (consulting, fintech, risk, generic SaaS), and iteratively removing the specific failure modes I kept seeing in the output. Deployed to Render with Docker.

What I learned

  • Cover-letter quality is upstream of generation. Forcing the model to do explicit business-problem identification before writing changes the output more than any post-generation editing prompt. Move the work upstream.
  • Anti-cliché rules have to be specific and enumerated. “Avoid clichés” doesn’t work. “Do not use the phrases X, Y, Z” works. The model is trained to write the clichés; you have to list them by name and block them.
  • Domain variants matter more than I expected. A generic prompt produces a generic letter. A prompt that knows the target is a credit-risk role (and therefore frames technical work as evidence of business judgment, not as the main story) produces a noticeably stronger letter.
  • The most useful product feature is the silent pre-analysis. Users don’t see it, but it’s why the output reads like a person wrote it.
